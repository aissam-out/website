## You Know Nothing, Jon Snow: The North Wall.

![The North Wall.](/media/you-know-nothing-the-north-wall/01-9b4311fc5c.png)

By now, certainty has had a rough journey.

We have already watched some of its favorite shelters weaken. The obvious world turned out to be less obvious than it looked. Science, that great modern refuge, turned out to be more human, more provisional, more exposed to error than its public image suggests. Even the senses, those loyal informants we trust so casually, were not handing us reality untouched. They were filtering, translating, constructing.

So it is natural, after all that, to retreat inward.

If the world outside is less stable than it seemed, if institutions can fail, if perception can mislead, then perhaps the final refuge is not out there at all. Perhaps it is here, in the one place that still feels immediate. The one thing that seems most intimately yours. The boss everyone is reporting to.

Your mind.

The thing that judges, compares, remembers, doubts, notices, corrects. The thing that, after every other disappointment, still feels like the last clean instrument left in the room.

And that would be comforting, if it were true.

## The Tilt

Think of how rarely an idea arrives in a clean room.

You hear a claim, and before you have really examined it, something in you has already moved. A slight sympathy. A slight resistance. Sometimes it is almost physical. This sounds right. That sounds stupid. This person seems serious. That person is obviously pushing something. One idea feels reasonable before you can explain why. Another feels wrong before you have fully understood it.

I notice this most clearly in smaller moments. In the instant irritation of being corrected. In how quickly I search for flaws in an argument once I dislike the person making it. In how noble my own motives can sound when I am the one narrating them.

And if you slow down enough, the process gets a little embarrassing.

A claim that flatters your prior view gets a softer landing. You give it time. You let it unfold. You look for what it might mean. A claim that threatens your picture gets a colder reception. You scrutinize the wording. You notice the tone. You become suddenly demanding. You ask for rigor, precision, evidence, standards you did not feel the need to invoke a minute earlier.

You can watch this happen in arguments, in politics, in friendships, in the private courtroom of your own head.

We like to imagine thinking as something that begins from nowhere. As if the mind first clears the room, puts reality on the table, and only then begins its patient inspection. But ordinary thought does not work like that. Serious thought does not fully work like that either.

A person wants to be right. A person wants not to look foolish. A person wants their loyalties intact, their identity coherent, their decisions justified, their pride preserved. None of this disappears the moment thought begins. It enters the process itself. It shapes what feels plausible, what feels offensive, what feels elegant, what feels obviously true, what feels too strange to accept.

And intelligence does not save you from this as much as people like to imagine.

Sometimes it only makes the performance more convincing.

A dull person may defend nonsense awkwardly. A sharper one can defend it beautifully. Better words. Better structure. Better camouflage. The conclusion remains what it was. It just acquires style, confidence, and citations.

And this is not some poetic suspicion about human weakness. It is one of the most studied things in psychology. I am just describing ordinary cognition under decent lighting.

And this does not only show up in obvious places like arguments, ideology, or personal loyalty. Variations of the same discomfort have appeared even in contexts designed to look cold and rational. There is a famous line of research around judicial decisions showing that outcomes can shift with things as mundane as fatigue, the timing of breaks, or the slow depletion of mental energy across a session.

There is a whole literature around motivated reasoning, confirmation bias, belief perseverance, identity-protective cognition. Different names, different emphases, same discomfort: the mind is not simply trying to find what is true. Very often, it is trying to preserve a shape it can continue living with.

There is also a quieter mistake the mind makes all the time: it confuses what happened with what it thinks it meant. A penguin walks away from the group, and within seconds people are no longer describing movement but sadness, exile, despair. But the fact was only the movement. The rest was projection. A short text becomes disrespect. Silence becomes judgment. A missed glance becomes dislike. The event is real, but the meaning arrives so fast that it starts to feel just as real.

And the strange part is not that this happens. The strange part is how clean it feels from the inside.

That is what makes it dangerous.

A distortion that feels like distortion is not that powerful. You can catch it. You can hesitate around it. But when a distortion arrives wearing the clothes of fairness, seriousness, or common sense, it gets farther. It gets to sit at the table as if it belonged there.

We talk a lot about deception as if it mostly came from the outside. Propaganda. Manipulation. Advertising. Ideology. But a frightening amount of the work is done internally, by a mind that knows how to make its own preferences sound principled.

And that should already weaken the picture.

## The Story You Keep Rewriting

Memory feels like the conservative faculty of the mind. The archivist. The keeper of what happened. You may misjudge things in the present, but memory, at least, gives continuity. It preserves. It keeps the thread.

But that is not really what it does.

> Memory is not a vault. It is closer to reconstruction.

This too is not a dramatic metaphor. It is one of the central findings of modern psychology and neuroscience. For decades, researchers have shown how memory is shaped by suggestion, framing, repetition, emotion, and later information. People can be led to remember details that were never there. Entire events can be recalled with confidence and vividness despite having been distorted or even implanted.

A family story gets told enough times, and eventually you remember it from the telling rather than from the event. The scene becomes smoother, more coherent, more narratable. Certain details harden. Others disappear. You begin to “remember” moments from the version that survived conversation, not necessarily from the day itself.

The mind dislikes loose stories. It prefers trajectories, patterns that seem to have been there all along. Once you know how something ended, the past begins to reorganize itself around that ending. A betrayal starts to cast earlier shadows. A failure begins to look announced. What was once ordinary becomes a sign. What was once ambiguous becomes a warning.

> Memory is often less interested in preserving the past than in making it narratable.

Elizabeth Loftus built much of her work around this uncomfortable fact: memory is not merely weak. It is editable.

So? How much is the past, and how much is the present reaching backward and rearranging it?

And confidence survives this remarkably well.

The mind does not merely remember badly. It remembers badly while preserving the feeling that it remembers well. It gives you not only an altered scene, but the conviction that the scene is intact.

## │Imagination│

Try to imagine something radically outside your experience. Not just unusual. Not exotic. Not unfamiliar in a cosmetic way. Something truly outside the kind of world your mind was built to handle.

It is harder than it sounds.

When fiction tries to imagine alien intelligence, it usually gives you a human with modifications. Bigger eyes. Stranger skin. Different limbs. A distorted face. But still recognizably the kind of thing a human mind can stage, because it is still borrowing from bodies, intentions, faces, communication, conflict, desire.

No one naturally imagines an alien as a smell. Or as a temporary relation between forces. Or as something that does not occupy the kind of boundary we would even call a thing.

And even those examples are still coming from me. They are human attempts to point beyond the human, using concepts that never really escape it.

So the problem is not only that the world may contain more than we know. The problem is that it may contain forms of existence, explanations, truths, meanings, that our imagination is not even shaped to generate.

That limitation is easy to underestimate because imagination feels infinite from the inside.

But it may be less like an open sky and more like a room with clever mirrors.

And then there is language.

> But before going there, let me slow down for a moment. I do not want this argument to harden into the wrong shape. I am not saying that the mind is useless, or that thought and reason have led us nowhere. Only that they have edges. And I find this strangely beautiful. But that part belongs to what comes next. For now, it is enough to stay here a little longer, where the fracture is still opening.

## The Cage That Helps You Think

Language feels like a transparent medium. You think something, then words come out. Simple.

But that is not really what happens.

Very often, words do not simply express thought. They organize it. They trim it. They harden it. They make certain distinctions available and others awkward. They allow some experiences to become clear while flattening others into rough approximations.

You can feel this in the way languages and cultures do not always cut the world at the same joints. One language gives a clear outline to something another leaves scattered in experience. I remember feeling this when I first encountered the Japanese word komorebi: the fleeting shimmer of sunlight filtering through leaves. Such an ordinary event, so light it might pass without leaving a mark. And yet once it had a name, it seemed to step forward from the background. Since then, I have felt a quiet joy each time I notice it, as if the word had taught me to see something that had always been there, waiting just below the threshold of attention. That is what language can do. It does not merely label experience after the fact. Sometimes it gathers it, steadies it, brings it into focus. And once a culture has words for certain distinctions, its speakers do not just speak differently. They begin, in subtle ways, to notice differently too.

But that gift comes at a cost.

> Language is useful because it is brutal.

> It compresses.

It takes an untidy spread of sensation, ambiguity, transition, resemblance, context, and turns it into something stable enough to point at. This is a chair. That is anger. That is justice.

And naming does something else too. It strips away a frightening amount of particularity. There is no such thing, in reality, as “a dog.” There is only this dog: this body, this age, this smell, this history, this posture in this light on this afternoon. The word is useful precisely because it erases most of that. It collapses a singular thing into a category. And we need that collapse. Without it, we could not generalize, predict, remember efficiently, communicate, or move through the world without having to meet every new case as if reality had just begun. But the gain in usefulness comes with a loss in fidelity. The category helps you think by teaching you to ignore.

Maybe that is why, when we try to express something deep, we rely on words that blur rather than sharpen. Light. Meaning. Home. Words that resist the tight grip of definition, and would lose something if they submitted to it. At that depth, recognition replaces explanation.

This is well studied too, though in different ways across linguistics, cognitive science, philosophy of language, and psychology. The exact claims vary, but the broad point survives all the nuance: language does not merely label a pre-cut reality. It participates in how that reality is carved for thought.

If you want one clean example to hold onto, look up the Russian blues study, where Russian speakers are quicker to tell apart light and dark blue because their language names them separately.

That matters more than it seems.

Because language is not merely the clothing of thought. It helps thought form. It cuts the world into units, makes some patterns easier to grasp, and some distinctions easier to keep stable. Which means its limits are not only expressive. They are structural. The danger is not just that language may lead you to describe reality badly. It is that its simplifications may begin to pass for reality itself.

So now the mind is in real trouble.

It does not begin neutrally. It does not preserve faithfully. It does not imagine freely. It does not even think through a perfectly clear medium.

And then there was the last refuge I was most reluctant to touch.

## The Cracks in the Cleanest Systems

Strict systems matter enormously. Mathematics, logic, formal proof, computation, all of that is a monument to what disciplined reasoning can do. And part of what makes them so powerful is that they keep forcing the same humiliation: what feels obvious is often not what is true.

At ordinary scale, the mind becomes attached to pictures of how reality should behave. Then physics arrives and starts offending those instincts. Quantum mechanics gives you particles described in ways that do not fit the furniture of common sense. Interference, superposition, entanglement: not just strange results, but a direct insult to the kind of world intuition keeps trying to stage. And this was not merely uncomfortable for amateurs. Even Einstein, faced with this, recoiled at what it seemed to demand. “God does not play dice,” he said, as if the theory had not only challenged a model of physics, but violated something deeper in thought’s sense of what reality ought to be.

Mathematics does something similar, but even more coldly. It tells you that taking things away from a set does not necessarily make it smaller. It tells you that a series with infinitely many positive terms can still add up to a finite number. It tells you that there are shapes with finite areas and infinite boundaries. Again and again, formal reasoning forces the same admission: what feels obvious to the mind is not a law of reality, only a habit formed at human scale.

And this matters here because formal systems are not some unrelated detour away from the mind. They are one of its highest expressions. They are what thought builds when it grows suspicious of its own looseness. When intuition feels compromised, when language feels slippery, when ordinary judgment seems too exposed to bias and distortion, the mind does not simply give up. It tries to discipline itself. It tries to replace instinct with structure, ambiguity with rules, intuition with proof. Formalism is not outside this story. It is the mind’s most serious attempt to escape its own frailty.

So perhaps that is the answer. Let intuition be local and unreliable. Let common sense be provincial. We can leave it behind. We can submit ourselves to stricter methods, cleaner symbols, explicit rules. If the ordinary mind cannot secure truth, perhaps formalism can. Perhaps there can exist a system so clear that, once you step inside it, nothing true within it can remain forever out of reach. A universe with no unreachable truths.

And then Gödel enters the scene. Not with another oddity, but with a far deeper wound:

> In any sufficiently rich formal system, there are truths that cannot be proven from within the system itself.

It is hard for me to explain how shattering that is.

The shock is not only in the sentence. It is in the setting. In formal structure itself. In mathematics. The very domain many hoped might finally become complete, self-grounding, and secure. Gödel did not expose a weakness in our habits of thought. He exposed a limit in one of our most disciplined attempts to escape those habits altogether.

> It was a crack in one of our oldest intellectual hopes: that if rigor were pushed far enough, truth and proof would finally coincide. They would not.

This was not the kind of result you absorb and move on from unchanged. The people who had believed that rigor might one day close the system from within were now forced to look at a limit written into the very thing they trusted most. Something had shifted. The temple was still standing, but one of its oldest promises no longer could.

Even here, in this cold and beautiful place, the walls are still there.

At some point the pattern becomes difficult to ignore.

The issue is not that humans are occasionally wrong. The issue is that every layer you hoped would rescue the previous one arrives carrying its own limitations.

> You keep digging for bedrock and keep finding architecture.

## Beyond the wall

Up to now, most of the pressure has been on thought itself, on reasoning, on the structures we use to justify and explain. And that is necessary. But there is a quiet assumption hiding there: that the mind, because it reasons, is the final authority on what is real or meaningful. That whatever cannot be stabilized in logic is somehow less serious. I do not think that holds.

The mind is not a neutral judge standing outside the process, calmly evaluating reality from above. It is part of the machinery: The heart pumps. The stomach digests. The lungs breathe. The brain thinks. That is its function. A central one, but still a function. But to say that thinking is its job is not to say that thinking exhausts reality, or that whatever exceeds it is unreal. In fact, some of the most undeniable things in life arrive precisely in that excess.

Music can undo you before you have a sentence for what it did. A friend touches your shoulder in a moment of grief or fear, and something in the structure of the world changes instantly, before any explanation appears. The mind comes later and does what it always does: it sorts, names, reduces, tries to make the event legible. Fine. Let it. But the event did not wait for the explanation to become true.

Reason is one instrument. A powerful one. Perhaps the most disciplined one we have. But we have spent four posts watching it reach its limits, not from outside, not from cynicism, but from the inside. From its own findings. The replication crisis is science discovering its own fallibility. The bias literature is the mind catching itself mid-distortion. And Gödel is something colder and more final than either: a proof, derived entirely within formal reasoning, that formal reasoning cannot close itself. That there are truths which exist but which no chain of proof will ever reach. Not because we lack the intelligence. Because of the structure of the system itself.

That is not a small result to absorb. It means that even if you were infinitely patient, infinitely rigorous, infinitely free of bias, even in that impossible version of yourself, the instrument would still have a ceiling. There would still be something on the other side of it.

The space of reality is larger than reason can map. Which means there could be other modes of access, and they are not competing with reason for the same territory. They may be pointing at a different part of the map entirely. One that reason, by its own admission, cannot reach.

So, the wall is still there. But now it no longer looks like the edge of reality. It looks more like the edge of one way of reaching it.
